The three fights that decide the round

  1. Net vs. gross. Consumer surplus is net of price but gross of unpriced social cost. CON wins by extracting the concession and repeating it; PRO wins by conceding the category first and demanding a same-scale deduction.
  2. Which segment the ballot weighs. CON wants the dependent, location-based core; PRO wants the supplementary majority plus consumers. Whoever wins this wins the round.
  3. The counterfactual. Is the alternative "the same work with a floor" (CON, and the resolution's own stated comparison) or "no paid transaction at all" (PRO, and the empirical reality for excluded workers)? PRO should press "name the employer."

Known evidence cautions

  • Do not run "435 million" as a first-access count. It is an upper-bound engagement figure including secondary and marginal workers. The first-access claim runs on the smaller main-activity core.

PRO case

PRO CASE — On balance, the rise of the gig economy has been more beneficial than harmful

FRAMEWORK

"On balance" is a weighing instruction, not an existence test. Four rules before the contentions.

One — scope. The resolution says "the gig economy," not "gig workers." The unit includes workers, consumers, small businesses, and public finances. The other side does not get to rename the topic.

Two — the counterfactual. We compare gig work to the same person's actual next-best option, not to a good formal job that was never offered. Every harm claim must name the world it is comparing to.

Three — segmentation. We concede the dependent full-time minority now: low net pay, missing protections, algorithmic pressure. Those harms are real for that slice. The ballot question is whether that minority harm outweighs the majority and consumer gains.

Four — the mechanisms. We weigh on scope, prerequisite, and reversibility. Magnitude counts only with a number — an adjective has zero weight. And severity claims require weights: if the other side says a harm is concentrated, they must produce the per-person figures that make the comparison, not assert intensity.

CONTENTION 1: DISTRIBUTION — the harm reaches a minority, the benefit reaches the majority

CLAIM. Gig work is overwhelmingly supplementary, so the dependent-precarity story describes a minority of participants, not the modal one.

COUNTERFACTUAL. The other side's harm assumes the representative gig worker is a dependent full-timer. That distribution claim is testable, and it fails.

LINK / EVIDENCE. Pew Research Center surveyed 10,348 U.S. adults in 2021 on a nationally representative panel: 68% of current and recent gig workers call it a side job; 31% call it their main job. Hours confirm it — 41% work under 10 hours in a typical week and 23% don't do these jobs most weeks, so roughly 64% work under 30 hours. Only 8% exceed 30. ◈Pew 2021: 68% of current/recent U.S. gig platform workers …

WARRANTS. - W1 — Majority supplement, not depend. Two-thirds are topping up income, not sourcing benefits or a full-time living from the platform. - W2 — Hours corroborate the self-report. A 64%-under-30-hours distribution is hard to reconcile with full-time misclassification as the representative case. - W3 — Exit is real. Nearly a quarter skip most weeks — the relationship is escapable.

IMPACT. Weigh on scope and reversibility. We concede the 31% core fully and put it on the scale. Against it sit the supplementary majority plus consumers. To win, the other side needs a per-person magnitude large enough to swamp both — and severity language is not that number.

CONTENTION 2: ACCESS — the platform is the first paid transaction for workers formal labor markets exclude

CLAIM. For workers the formal labor market never hired, the platform is not a worse job; it is the only paid transaction on offer.

COUNTERFACTUAL. Absent platforms, the populations reached are not sitting in protected formal employment. The World Bank finds that in low-income countries "over 90 percent of the workforce does not contribute to social insurance and remains outside the purview of labor regulations." ◈World Bank 2023 (Con-favorable, recorded for baseline use)… The unprotected status predates the app — so the alternative is informal work, not a lost good job.

LINK / EVIDENCE. The ILO finds digital labour platforms grew almost fivefold over the decade to 2021, and that they are "opening up opportunities that did not exist before, particularly for women, young people, persons with disabilities and marginalized groups." ◈ILO: digital labour platforms grew fivefold in a decade an…

SEGMENT — state this precisely. The World Bank puts online gig work at up to 12% of the global labor market and up to 435 million people, "often as secondary employment." ◈World Bank 2023: online gig work is up to 12% of the globa… That 435 million is an upper-bound engagement count, not a count of first paid transactions. The first-access claim runs on the main-gig core — workers for whom the platform is the principal paid activity. The secondary tail supports Contention 1, not this one. Do not merge the two numbers.

WARRANTS. - W1 — The growth is real and measured: a fivefold decade increase is the agreed size of the change. - W2 — Additionality: the ILO's own language is that these opportunities "did not exist before" — the platform is the first transaction, not a degraded version of a prior job. - W3 — It lands where the alternative is weakest: platform demand grew 130% in sub-Saharan Africa against 14% in North America, 2016–2020. ◈World Bank 2023: online gig work is up to 12% of the globa…

IMPACT. Weigh on prerequisite. For the core, removing the platform removes the income — not merely lowers it. A prerequisite benefit outranks a harm that exists only because a real option now exists.

ALTERNATE CONTENTION: CONSUMER AND THIRD-PARTY VALUE

Use this to widen scope, never as the lead. It is the most attackable contention in the file, and the attack is predictable — see the warning below.

CLAIM. Consumers captured large, measured value from platform matching. EVIDENCE. Cohen et al., NBER Working Paper 22627 (2016), estimate from "almost 50 million individual-level observations and a regression discontinuity design" that UberX generated about $1.60 of consumer surplus per $1 spent and roughly $6.8 billion in U.S. consumer surplus in 2015. ◈Cohen et al. 2016: UberX generated about $2.9B consumer su…

⚠ KNOWN VULNERABILITIES — read before deploying. 1. Affiliation. Two authors held Uber equity and the data is Uber's. Disclose it yourself and discount to ~$3.4B rather than being caught defending the full figure. 2. Consumer surplus is not net social benefit. It is net of price but gross of unpriced social cost. A prepared opponent will say "net beats gross" and win the comparison. Do not enter this as a net welfare figure. Enter it as one measured component and force the other side to quantify the deduction they want to make. 3. It cannot carry the case alone. If this is your only dollar figure, the case rests on a number you have already conceded is incomplete.

ALTERNATE: FLEXIBILITY VALUE

MANDATORY DISCLOSURE — do not deploy this card without saying this out loud. The flexibility-surplus study uses Uber-supplied data, one author was Uber-employed and held equity, and another held stock options. All of it is disclosed in the paper itself, which means a prepared opponent can read the disclosure back at you and make it look like concealment. Say it first, in the same breath as the number: "this is Uber's own data, disclosed, so treat it as contested — and even discounted it still shows positive worker-side value." Disclosing costs you a little weight. Getting caught costs you the evidence judge, and it costs the credibility of every other number you read. (This is a rule, not advice: it was followed in one round, dropped in the next when the team changed, and the drop was caught by the evidence ballot.)

Available, but the supporting literature is also platform-affiliated. Treat as contested, not clean, and never as the sole worker-side benefit.

CASE STRATEGY

Collapse plan. Funnel every speech into two comparisons: (A) distribution — the supplementary majority plus consumers against the conceded dependent minority; and (B) prerequisite — first access for excluded workers, run on the main-gig core. Do not relitigate the minority harm. Concede it and weigh it.

Concession discipline — the most important line in this file. Concession is a weighing tool, not a credibility tool. Concede what you cannot defend; do not concede what merely looks contestable. A case that concedes its own evidence's weight, its scope figure, and its dollar number keeps its honesty and loses its offense. Every concession must buy a weighing advantage you then actually cash.

ADAPTIVE TREE. - IF opp runs misclassification / precarity → lead Contention 1, concede the dependent core, overweigh on the supplementary majority plus consumers. - IF opp runs net earnings are poverty-level → do not defend a net-earnings number we do not have. Force the counterfactual: low compared to what? Then run Contention 2. - IF opp runs consumer surplus is a transfer → do not lead the surplus contention. Concede it is gross of unpriced cost, demand they quantify the deduction at the same scale as the benefit, and pivot to distribution and access. - IF opp runs concentration / severity ("a dollar lost by a low-earning worker matters more than a dollar saved by a consumer") → this is the argument most likely to win them the ballot, and it is answerable. Do not concede that concentration is a weighing mechanism on its own. Run "produce the weights": if they want to reweight headcount by stake, they must supply per-person and aggregate loss figures for both the dependent core and the supplementary tail. Without those, concentration is an intensifier with no arithmetic. Then note that the supplementary majority contains a substantial reliance tail — it is not a caucus of people losing pocket money. - IF opp runs the employment counterfactual ("the alternative is the same work with a floor") → press for the employer. If no employer class would have created those transactions, the floor is a benchmark, not an option those workers held. - IF opp contests scale itself → return to the framework: "on balance" is a weighing instruction, and they must weigh, not merely list harms.

CON case

CON CASE — On balance, the rise of the gig economy has been more harmful than beneficial

FRAMEWORK — weigh the dependent segment against the employment counterfactual

"On balance" is a weighing instruction, so name what we weigh and against what before any contention.

We segment, and we concede honestly. The online, high-skill, supplementary upside is real and we grant it. The ballot should weigh the dependent, location-based segment — the ride-hail driver, the delivery rider, the Meituan courier — because that is where the model's promise, flexibility, is tested against its risk: dependent work without the rights of employment.

The comparison is fixed by the resolution itself. It is not gig work against a utopia, and not gig work against nothing. It is this same work under a traditional employment relationship. Against that baseline, the gig economy is a transfer, not a creation — and on balance a transfer scores zero, because moving value is not creating it.

CONTENTION 1: TRANSFER, NOT CREATION

CLAIM. The gig economy did not remove the costs of employment. It relocated them onto workers and the public.

COUNTERFACTUAL. Under an employment relationship the firm carries the capital and the social contributions for every hour worked.

ATTRIBUTION — this is the causal step, and it is the strongest link in the file. The mechanism is classification, not technology. The ILO's 2021 study of Chinese platforms finds terms of service "treat platform workers as 'self-employed,'" and that "by virtue of their self-employed status, platform workers are thus excluded from many labour protections and social welfare provisions." ◈ILO China: platform workers classified as self-employed ar… Nothing about an app requires this; the reclassification does the shifting.

SEGMENT. Dependent, location-based platform workers — the ones whose rent depends on the platform, not a top-up.

WARRANTS. - W1 — The protection gap. The ILO's 2021 global survey finds "the majority of the workers on digital labour platforms lack social protection coverage." ◈ILO platform earnings and social protection - W2 — The gap is lived, not technical. In the same survey, 7 in 10 platform workers said they could not take paid sick leave or receive compensation if they tested positive. ◈ILO platform earnings and social protection - W3 — The public pays the bill. In Massachusetts alone, the state auditor found Uber and Lyft avoided "more than $266 million" into workers' compensation, unemployment insurance, and paid family leave over ten years — $47 million in 2023 alone. ◈Massachusetts State Auditor (via Teamsters release): Uber/… In California, the same two firms would have paid an estimated $413 million into the UI fund, 2014–2019. ◈UC Berkeley Labor Center: Uber/Lyft shifted $413M in UI co…

IMPACT. The cheap ride is not new value; it is a price cut funded by someone else's risk. The worker supplies the car, the fuel, and the unpaid waiting time; the public absorbs the social-insurance bill the platform no longer pays. Weigh on scope and timeframe: the protection gap covers the majority of platform workers globally, today, and the fiscal bill compounds every year the model grows.

CONTENTION 2: THE PAY FLOOR DISAPPEARS

CLAIM. The access the other side celebrates is access to sub-minimum pay after expenses.

COUNTERFACTUAL. Under employment, minimum-wage law binds the firm for every hour worked, including waiting time.

ATTRIBUTION. Platforms pay by the task and count only engaged time, while the worker carries the expenses — so the floor is evaded by design, not by bad luck. This is not a downturn story: the gap appears in the very cities whose legal floor is highest.

WARRANTS. - W1 — The net number. UC Berkeley, 2024, from 52,000 trips by nearly 1,100 drivers across five metros: most app drivers earn "significantly less than minimum wage when all work time, gas, and vehicle wear and tear are factored in" — California passenger drivers a median $5.97/hour before tips, delivery drivers $4.98, against a $16.00 minimum. ◈UC Berkeley: gig drivers earn sub-minimum wages after all … This figure is already net of the driver's own costs — say so, because it is the property the other side's headline number lacks. - W2 — Not a U.S. anomaly. Fairwork (Oxford, 2023): only 2 of 12 major UK platforms could prove their workers earn minimum wage after costs. ◈Fairwork/Oxford: most UK platforms fail minimum-wage and f… - W3 — The global floor. The ILO 2021 finds half of online platform workers earn under $2/hour. ◈ILO 2021: half of online platform workers earn under US$2/…

IMPACT. This is the direct answer to the access story. Access to sub-minimum pay after expenses is not opportunity — it is the mechanism by which platforms undercut employers. Weigh on magnitude and timeframe: sub-floor earnings are immediate and measured, and they scale with the very growth the other side celebrates.

CASE STRATEGY

Collapse plan. Collapse to Contention 1 with the pay floor folded inside it. The single named mechanism is net beats gross: the other side's benefit figure is a consumer-side, gross number; ours are net and below the legal floor. One contention serviced properly beats four mechanisms asserted.

The strongest weighing move — and its known weakness. Concentration: a dollar lost by a driver earning $4.98 an hour is rent and food; a dollar saved by a rider is convenience. This wins ballots across lay and technical judges alike, and it should be run. But a prepared opponent will demand the weights — per-person and aggregate loss figures for the dependent core and the supplementary tail — and we do not currently have them. Those hourly gaps are the arithmetic; lead with them the moment weights are demanded.

Refuse cleanly. Do not invent a national or global cost total. Saying "I have two state figures and I won't manufacture a third" costs magnitude and buys credibility — and it makes everything else we claim believable.

Topic analysis — Gig economy, on balance

Resolution: Resolved: On balance, the rise of the gig economy has been more beneficial than harmful. Source: NHSDLC (National High School Debate League of China). Revised by the league from its original framing, "…has been beneficial to the global labor market." Slug: gig-economy-on-balance Status: LIVE COMPETITIVE TOPIC. Not a simulation. Full-quality prep. Private: no .public marker, and .gitignore ignores topics/* by default. This topic must never be published while it is live.

0. TOPIC TYPE — read this before writing anything

This is an ON-BALANCE / COMPARATIVE-TREND topic. It is NOT a policy topic. The system's prior topic (war powers) was a policy resolution and every agent here has to un-learn that shape. Concretely:

  • There is no plan, no fiat, no actor. Nobody "does" anything in the Pro world. The resolution asks whether a trend that already happened has been net good.
  • Therefore there is no solvency and no inherency. Do not write a "solvency" contention. Do not attack "solvency." Those stock issues do not exist here. An agent that runs them has misread the topic and will be marked down.
  • The burden is symmetric and comparative (the league says so explicitly): Pro must show benefits outweigh harms; Con must show harms outweigh benefits. Neither side defends a perfect world. Con does not have to prove the gig economy is a catastrophe; Con has to win the net comparison. Pro does not have to deny any harm exists.
  • The counterfactual is the whole ballgame. The comparison is: the status quo gig economy vs. a hypothetical world where more of this work is done under a traditional employment relationship. Every impact claim must be run against that counterfactual, not against an imaginary utopia. See §2 — this is the most important section in the file.

What replaces uniqueness/link/solvency: the contention structure in WRITING-STANDARD.md still applies, but read "UNIQUENESS" as the counterfactual baseline (what the world looks like without the gig economy's rise) and "LINK" as attribution (proof the gig economy caused this outcome rather than coinciding with it). See the on-balance adaptation section in WRITING-STANDARD.md.

1. Terms of art — where the definitional fights are

  • "The gig economy" — scope fight. Narrow read: app-mediated, algorithmically managed, per-task work (Uber, DiDi, Meituan, DoorDash, Upwork, Fiverr). Broad read: all freelance/contingent work. Pro generally wants broad (sweeps in well-paid professional freelancers, consultants, online creatives). Con generally wants narrow (concentrates the debate on location-based platform labor, where the harms cluster). Neither side should let the other define this uncontested.
  • "The rise" — the resolution indicts the growth of the model, not the existence of freelancing. The ILO's fivefold growth in digital labour platforms 2010–2020 is the agreed fact of change. Useful Pro framing: freelance work is ancient; what "rose" is matching efficiency. Useful Con framing: what "rose" is the reclassification of dependent work as contracting.
  • "On balance" — a weighing instruction, not a threshold. It licenses and demands explicit comparative weighing. A team that never weighs has not met the resolution's own standard. This is the single best framework hook on the topic for both sides.
  • "Beneficial / harmful" — to whom? The league deliberately widened this beyond the labor market to include consumers, small businesses, public finances, and society. Pro should press this: a labor-market-only lens is the old, rejected wording. Con should answer that consumer surplus bought with worker risk-transfer is not a net benefit, just a transfer — see §2, the transfer-vs-creation axis.

2. THE CENTRAL ANALYTIC AXIS — creation vs. transfer

Everything on this topic reduces to one question, and the team that controls it wins:

Did the gig economy CREATE value, or MOVE value from workers and public systems to platforms and consumers?

  • Pro's core structural claim: creation. The transactions would not otherwise exist. Matching efficiency turns idle assets and idle hours into paid work; a ride, a delivery, a translation that no firm would have hired for now happens. If the transaction is genuinely additional, the gains are real and not taken from anyone.
  • Con's core structural claim: transfer. Cheap rides and fast delivery are cheap and fast partly because the worker supplies the car, fuel, phone, insurance, and unpaid waiting time, and because the public absorbs the social-insurance costs the employer no longer pays. A lower price funded by an uncompensated risk shift is a transfer wearing the costume of efficiency.

Both can be partly true, and the honest answer is that the mix differs by segment. That is why §3 matters. The winning team is the one that shows its mechanism dominates in the segments that carry the most weight — not the one that asserts its mechanism is the only one operating.

Second axis — agency vs. repackaged precarity (the league's own closing framing): did the model add a genuine choice, or did it gradually make precarious work the only choice? Pro wants "additional option, revealed preference." Con wants "the outside option decayed, so 'choice' is measured against a shrinking alternative."

3. SEGMENTATION — the required move on this topic

The league's briefing insists on it and the best debates run on it. A team arguing about "gig workers" as one undifferentiated group is arguing badly. Three cuts:

Cut Pro's strong ground Con's strong ground
Online vs. location-based online/remote (Upwork, Fiverr): global market access, high-skill, real earnings ceiling, genuine autonomy location-based (ride-hail, delivery): capital costs on the worker, physical risk, algorithmic control, thin margins
Supplementary vs. primary income supplementary: smoothing, bridging job loss, caregivers/students; a small top-up cannot be "exploitation" primary/dependent: rent depends on the platform, so formal freedom to log off is not real freedom
High- vs. low-skill high-skill: scarce talent, real bargaining power, portfolio careers low-skill: substitutable labor, oversupply drives rates down, no career ladder

Strategic consequence. Pro should concede the worst location-based/dependent cases narrowly and argue the distribution — most participation is supplementary, and the counterfactual for the marginal worker is unemployment, not a good job. Con should concede the online/high-skill upside narrowly and argue the weight — the dependent location-based segment is where the volume and the human stakes are, and it is the segment the model is growing into. Whoever wins "which segment should the ballot weigh" wins the round. Prepare this as the framework fight, not an afterthought.

4. Ground assessment

Genuinely balanced — this is a well-cut topic. The league re-wrote it specifically to equalize ground, and it worked.

  • Pro gets: the strongest empirical foundation (participation is overwhelmingly voluntary and supplementary in most measured populations), the revealed-preference argument, consumer and small-business surplus, market access for people formal labor markets exclude (women, caregivers, students, rural, migrants), and the "even bad work beats unemployment" floor — which is the hardest single argument for Con to answer head-on. Pro's job: make the counterfactual concrete and unemployment-shaped.
  • Con gets: the sharpest normative frame (dependent work stripped of the rights of employment), documented risk transfer, the ILO finding that most platform workers lack social-security coverage, algorithmic management as unaccountable control, and the strongest long-run argument — erosion of the institutions (training, career ladders, social insurance, collective bargaining) that make work sustainable. Con's job: make the timeframe long and the counterfactual employment-shaped.
  • The trap for Pro: relying on "flexibility" as a terminal value. Con's risk-transfer argument is precisely the answer to it — flexibility is the thing sold in exchange for the protections. Pro must convert flexibility into a quantified access/earnings claim or it dies.
  • The trap for Con: arguing gig work is bad in isolation. The comparison is not "gig job vs. good job," it is "gig job vs. that worker's actual alternative." Con needs evidence on what the alternative really was, or Pro's floor argument wins by default.
  • The trap for both: the algorithmic-management debate is easy to argue and hard to quantify. It is Con's most rhetorically powerful and evidentially thinnest ground.

5. Quantification — this topic is DATA-RICH (note the change)

The war-powers topic was a law/procedure topic where WRITING-STANDARD.md's numbers-are-scarce exception applied. That exception does NOT apply here. This is one of the most heavily measured questions in labor economics. A contention without a number on this topic is underwritten, and the evidence judge will say so.

Hunt for: share of workers on platforms; share for whom it is primary vs. supplementary income; hourly earnings net of expenses (the single most contested number on the topic — gross vs. net is where most miscut cards live); expense/capital burden; hours; turnover and duration; social-insurance coverage rates; consumer surplus estimates; wage effects on adjacent traditional sectors; platform take-rate over time.

Evidence caution, in advance: take-rate and net-earnings figures are frequently sourced from platform-funded studies. Both sides must check funding and method. A platform-commissioned net-earnings number cited as neutral is an INDICT waiting to happen — and if we cut it, it is an INDICT waiting to happen to us.

6. Regional scope — NHSDLC-specific, do not skip

This is a Chinese league, and the resolution's own examples name DiDi and Meituan alongside the Western platforms. Consequences:

  • Prep must not be US-only. The resolution says "the rise of the gig economy," unmodified by country — it is a global claim, which cuts both ways: Con can reach for the weakest-protection jurisdictions, Pro can reach for the developing-market access story (ILO and World Bank both document platform work as an entry point where formal jobs are scarce).
  • Chinese platform-labor scale is large and is the most concrete instantiation available to a judge in this league. Research must cover it, not treat it as an aside.
  • Judge-facing: examples a Chinese high-school judge can picture (a Meituan rider) will land harder than a US-regulatory example (AB5, Prop 22). Keep the US regulatory fight as evidence, not as the narrative.

7. Research priorities for r1 (ranked)

  1. ILO — digital labour platform reports: the fivefold-growth figure (2010–2020), social-protection coverage rates, earnings/hours data. Highest-authority source on the topic and cited by the league's own briefing. Both sides need it.
  2. Net vs. gross earnings — the contested core number, with method and funding noted.
  3. Primary vs. supplementary distribution — the number that decides §3's framework fight. Pro needs it; Con needs the dependent-segment counter-number.
  4. The counterfactual — what did platform workers actually do before / instead? Displacement vs. additionality. This is the weakest-evidenced and most decisive link on the topic for BOTH sides.
  5. Social-insurance / public-finance effects — Con's transfer claim needs a fiscal number.
  6. Consumer + small-business surplus — Pro's widened-scope ground per the new wording.
  7. Algorithmic management — evidence beyond anecdote; Fairwork-style scored ratings.
  8. China / developing-market data — per §6.

8. Framework notes

Because "on balance" is a weighing instruction, the framework fight is unusually substantive here. Live options: - Net welfare / cost-benefit (either side; the default). - Weigh the worst-off / dependent worker (Con) — prioritarian; pairs with §3. - Revealed preference and the counterfactual (Pro) — people keep choosing it over their actual alternatives. - Structural / long-run institutional effects (Con) — timeframe weighing: today's convenience against tomorrow's eroded institutions. - Scope: consumers + small business + society, not just workers (Pro) — anchored in the league's own reason for the rewording, which makes it hard for Con to call abusive.

9. League briefing (verbatim source material, provided by the user)

The NHSDLC's own topic explanation, preserved because it is the authoritative statement of intended ground and both sides' judges may share its assumptions.

The gig economy describes paid work organized around separate tasks, rides, deliveries, or projects rather than a long-term employment relationship. Gig workers are typically classified as independent contractors or freelancers who get paid for each piece of work completed. Although freelance work is not new, the contemporary modern rise is connected to the popularity of digital platforms such as Uber, DiDi, Meituan, DoorDash, Upwork, and Fiverr. In general, these platforms match customers and workers, provide payment services, and often evaluate the worker's performance using ratings and algorithms. The ILO reports that the number of digital labor platforms grew fivefold between 2010 and 2020, showing how quickly this model has expanded.

Gig work covers very different experiences. A person who uses these platforms for some extra income is in a completely different situation than a delivery worker who depends on the platform to pay their rent. Debaters should therefore distinguish online and location-based work, primary and supplementary income, and high- and low-skill gigs. Overall, this is a debate about a trend, not about a new policy. The comparison is between the status quo of the gig economy and a hypothetical world where more of this "gig" work would be performed under a traditional employment relationship.

The NHSDLC originally framed the resolution as, "On balance, the rise of the gig economy has been beneficial to the global labor market." We revised it to, "On balance, the rise of the gig economy has been more beneficial than harmful." This clarification was made for two reasons. First, gig work affects not only the labor market but also consumers, small businesses, public finances, and the society at large. Second, we wanted to create an even more equal argumentative ground for both sides. The earlier labor-market framing naturally emphasized Pro arguments such as job creation and flexibility, while several important Con harms were harder to fit within that narrow label. The new wording provides equal burdens: Pro must show that the benefits outweigh the harms, while Con must show that the harms outweigh the benefits. It keeps the same subject while producing a fairer and more balanced debate.

Pro teams could argue access and flexibility. Gig economy jobs have lower entry barriers compared to formal employment. Anyone can simply sign up, showcase a certain skill and start earning money, skipping the usual lengthy process of being hired. Gigs workers may choose to work whenever they want to and for how long they want to, thus, gigs can be useful as supplementary income or a bridge between jobs. The ILO and World Bank find that such flexibility can be especially useful for young people, women, students, caregivers, and people who live in areas where formal jobs are scarce. Pro can use the fact that even bad work is better than unemployment.

Pro sides can also argue efficiency. Platforms save time and money needed for recruitment, payments, and allocation of labor to a certain place or project. Online platforms enable designers, translators, programmers, and tutors to reach their clients beyond local markets. Restaurants and retailers can provide their services to customers without creating their own delivery network, and customers get faster, more numerous and usually cheaper services. If these efficiencies create transactions that would not otherwise occur, Pro can claim benefits in productivity, entrepreneurship, skill development, and job creation.

Con teams' main point is that flexibility often comes from transferring risk from the company to the worker. In the gig economy, the gig worker will often provide the car, phone, computer, fuel, insurance, and unpaid time of waiting for their assignment. The income of the gig worker fluctuates with changes in demand and changes in the rate of pay by the platform. At the same time workers categorized as contractors do not have minimum wage laws, paid time off, unemployment benefits, safety insurance, pensions, and collective bargaining. According to the ILO, most digital platform workers lack social security protection. Con can therefore frame the model as dependent work without the rights of employment: consumers receive lower prices partly because workers and public systems absorb risks that employers previously carried.

Con can also argue algorithmic management. The ratings, acceptance rate, cancellation rate, and feedback may determine who receives the job and who stays active on a platform. While workers are formally allowed to reject an assignment, practically they are forced to accept work they wouldn't accept otherwise since rejecting it could affect their rating, bonuses, or chances of getting work in the future. Automated decisions are inherently hard to interpret and appeal. Apart from the individual aspect, replacing employees with less-protected contractors may weaken salaries, training programs, career development, and social-insurance systems throughout the economy. Con can argue that the gig economy creates convenience today by eroding the institutions that make work sustainable tomorrow.

Ultimately, the topic is not resolved by saying that flexibility is good or that exploitation is bad. Both claims can be true. The deeper question is whether the rise of the gig economy has expanded genuine economic agency, or whether it has repackaged insecure work as personal freedom while platforms retain the most important forms of control. The strongest debates will ask who captures the value created by the platform, who carries the risk when work disappears or accidents happen, and whether the model gives people an additional choice or gradually makes precarious work the only choice available.
