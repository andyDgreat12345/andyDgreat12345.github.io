### II. The Core Hulme Insight Is Invariant to Entry Conditions

PRO's entire attack rests on the claim that I'm "modeling the resolution as if it inserts a vote into Hulme's Model II." > "When acting pursuant to an AUMF, however, presidents no longer have to worry about LRCs and thus are willing to utilize more force. The president no longer has political exposure; they have political cover."

This mechanism -- shared responsibility -> LRC elimination -> reduced constraint on force scale -- is not an artifact of the president *choosing* to seek authorization. It is a consequence of Congress *granting* authorization. The moment lawmakers affix their names to the operation, they cannot "wash their hands" or "sit on the sidelines." Their political fortunes become tied to the operation's success. The LRC mechanism -- which depends on the president bearing *sole* responsibility -- is disabled. This holds whether authorization was sought optionally or mandated, whether the president wanted the vote or was forced into it. The effect of authorization on LRCs is identical.

PRO's argument would only work if mandatory authorization somehow changed what authorization *does*. It doesn't. An AUMF under the resolution is still an AUMF. Congress still shares responsibility. LRCs are still eliminated. The graduated ceiling still dissolves.

### III. PRO's "Binary vs. Graduated" Point Proves MY Case

PRO argues the resolution creates "a world of *binary* congressional commitment: yes (authorized, deploy) or no (not authorized, cannot deploy)." They claim this means I'm "solving the wrong equation."

But this is precisely the *definitional-relocation* problem. The resolution relocates constraint from a graduated mechanism (LRCs scaling with ?, creating continuous restraint on *f*) to a binary mechanism (a single yes/no vote). Under the binary model PRO concedes:

- **If Congress says no:** zero force. Total constraint -- but also total impotence if force is genuinely needed. This is not a *constraint* on executive war-making so much as a *prohibition* on executive war-making. It is the opposite extreme: paralysis, not calibrated restraint.

- **If Congress says yes:** LRCs are eliminated. The president has political cover. Force is unconstrained by congressional sentiment. The graduated ceiling -- the mechanism that Hulme's empirical data shows actually constrains the *scale* of real-world interventions -- is gone.

The resolution thus produces a bifurcated world: infinite constraint or zero constraint, with no graduated middle. This is worse for democratic constraint on the *conduct* of war than the status quo, where LRCs create continuous discipline on presidential decision-making even (especially) when the president acts alone. The floor (mandatory vote) destroys the ceiling (graduated LRC constraint) by replacing a spectrum with a switch.

### IV. The Fulbright Point, Properly Read

PRO claims Fulbright's mechanism "can't operate because there's no 'act alone' path where it could." This misreads both Fulbright and the resolution.

Fulbright's insight is that the *possibility* of acting alone -- and bearing concentrated responsibility -- is what disciplines presidents. The resolution removes this possibility. It doesn't "bypass" the LRC mechanism in a neutral way; it *abolishes the condition under which the LRC mechanism does its best work.* Under the resolution, the president never faces the Fulbright scenario -- never has to think "I'd better calibrate this carefully because if it goes wrong, I'm alone holding the bag." Instead, the president faces either (a) no action at all, or (b) action with full congressional cover and zero LRC exposure. The graduated caution that Fulbright praised is structurally eliminated.

This is not a "bypass" -- it's a demolition. The resolution doesn't route around the LRC mechanism; it removes the terrain on which the LRC mechanism operates.

### V. The Empirical Bottom Line

Hulme's core empirical finding is that "presidents only risk full-scale war when they have the political cover provided by formal authorization." The theory explains why: authorization eliminates LRCs, removing the graduated constraint that otherwise limits force scale. The resolution makes authorization *mandatory* -- meaning every full-scale war will now come with that political cover, and none will face the LRC ceiling. The resolution universalizes the very condition Hulme identifies as enabling maximum force. That is the floor destroying the ceiling -- not as a modeling artifact, but as a structural prediction about what mandatory authorization does to the graduated constraint mechanism.

PRO's attack replaces this structural argument with a game-tree taxonomy and then declares victory when the taxonomy shifts. But the underlying mechanism -- shared responsibility kills LRCs, LRCs are the ceiling, therefore mandatory shared responsibility kills the ceiling -- survives any permutation of the entry conditions. The floor destroys the ceiling because authorization, however triggered, dissolves the graduated constraint that Hulme shows is democracy's real check on the scale of war.

## DEFENDING (CON): definitional-relocation
