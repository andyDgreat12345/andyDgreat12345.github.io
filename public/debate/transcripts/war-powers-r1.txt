

## 01-constructive-1: technicians (CON) -- constructive

We oppose. The resolution fails twice -- once because it cannot work, and once because even if it could, the costs are unacceptable. Two contentions: solvency, then operational necessity. I'll preview the weighing now: solvency is the prior question. If the mechanism doesn't function, every Pro impact is irrelevant. That's where this round ends.

---

**CONTENTION 1: SOLVENCY -- A STATUTE CANNOT ELIMINATE A CONSTITUTIONAL POWER.**

Here's the chain. The resolution is a statute. It proposes to eliminate a presidential authority. But presidents across party lines claim that authority flows from Article II of the Constitution -- not from any congressional grant. A statute cannot override a constitutional claim. The resolution is a parchment barrier: it eliminates nothing.

The warrant: fifty years of non-compliance. Every president since Nixon has treated the War Powers Resolution -- a less restrictive statute than what Pro proposes -- as constitutionally suspect. The LII Constitution Annotated, updated through May 2025, states: "Aside from its use as a rhetorical device, the War Powers Resolution has been of little worth in reordering presidential-congressional relations in the years since its enactment. In general, Presidents operating under it have expressly or implicitly considered it to be at least in part an unconstitutional infringement on presidential powers, and on each occasion of use abroad of United States troops the President in reporting to Congress has done so 'consistent[ly] with' the reporting section but not pursuant to the provision."

Presidents don't just ignore the WPR -- they've built an architecture justifying why they don't need any statutory authority at all. The CRS Legal Sidebar from August 2024 -- five months ago -- documents the framework. The Obama and Trump administrations articulated a two-part test: the president may act without Congress when the action serves important national interests, and when it falls short of "war" -- meaning not prolonged and substantial, no exposure of U.S. personnel to significant risk over a substantial period.

And this isn't theoretical. The same CRS report documents that President Biden submitted five consecutive 48-hour reports between October and December 2023 for strikes in Iraq and Syria -- relying solely on inherent Article II power. Not the 2001 AUMF. Not the 2002 AUMF. Inherent constitutional authority alone.

The impact: terminal solvency takeout. The resolution proposes to eliminate an authority the executive branch claims is constitutionally inherent. Presidents will not comply with a statute they believe is unconstitutional -- and they have a fifty-year track record proving it. When George W. Bush signed the 2002 Iraq AUMF, he explicitly stated that his signing "does not constitute any change in the long-standing positions of the executive branch on either the President's constitutional authority to use force or on the constitutionality of the War Powers Resolution." Even when Congress authorizes force, the president reserves the claim he didn't need to ask. If the mechanism doesn't work, no Pro impact ever materializes.

---

**CONTENTION 2: OPERATIONAL NECESSITY -- SPEED AND SECRECY REQUIRE EXECUTIVE DISCRETION.**

Even if the resolution could bind the president -- which it cannot -- eliminating unilateral authority creates lethal operational rigidity. Congress cannot match the decision speed of a unitary executive, and sensitive operations require secrecy that congressional consultation destroys.

On speed: Saigon, April 1975. President Ford asked Congress for authorization to use forces for sixty days to defend the evacuation. The Senate approved on April 25. The House delayed debate until May 1. Ford, "frustrated and facing increasing military pressure to act, ordered the evacuation of Saigon on April 28." The Army War College concludes: "The point is, that even with strong evidence that action is needed, the deliberations of Congress don't always meet the operational necessity. The President must, and did, make the appropriate decision and take the necessary action, making the concern for technical compliance a secondary consideration."

This is structural -- not partisan, not circumstantial. A 535-member body with committees, floor debate, and bicameralism cannot decide in hours what a commander-in-chief decides in minutes.

On secrecy: Iran hostage rescue, 1980. President Carter -- a Democrat with a Democratic Congress -- chose not to consult before the mission. The administration's position: "prior consultations were not undertaken in order to insure security of the operation." Congress itself acknowledged the logic -- the CRS confirms that "Congress approved the Gang of Eight notification provision in 1980 following a failed covert operation to rescue American embassy hostages in Iran. Congressional approval came after President Jimmy Carter decided not to notify the intelligence committees of the operation in advance because of concerns over operational security and the risk of disclosure."

The Gang of Eight is notification, not approval. Pro's resolution eliminates even that -- it requires full congressional authorization before any deployment.

The impact: the resolution forces every president to choose between breaking the law and letting Americans die. Ford chose Saigon. Carter chose the rescue. These weren't rogue presidents -- they were commanders-in-chief making decisions that Congress structurally cannot make in time. The resolution doesn't prevent these operations. It makes lawbreakers of the presidents who conduct them. That is not a solution.

---

**WEIGHING.**

Judge, one question decides this round: can a statute eliminate a constitutional power? For fifty years, across nine administrations of both parties, the answer has been no. Presidents claim Article II authority. They act on it. They route around statutes. The resolution adds one more statute to the pile. Solvency comes first -- and Pro cannot win it. Vote Con.


## 02-constructive-2: narrators (PRO) -- constructive

The Founders didn't trust kings. They'd just fought a revolution to get rid of one.

So when they wrote the Constitution, they made one choice with what James Madison called "studied care." They gave the power to make war to Congress -- not to the president. Why? Because "the Executive is the branch of power most interested in war, and most prone to it."

One person should never decide alone to send other people's children to die. That is the principle. For seventy years, we have abandoned it.

My opponents just told you two things: a statute can't bind the president, and Congress is too slow. They want you to believe the Constitution is a dead letter and the Founders' machine can't be fixed. Here's why they're wrong.

---

**CONTENTION 1: THE DESIGN**

The Constitution's assignment of war powers was not ambiguous. It was a deliberate rejection of monarchy.

Saikrishna Prakash -- conservative originalist, clerked for Clarence Thomas, chaired professor at UVA Law -- reviewed every founding-era source. His conclusion: "By taking the international law concept of 'declaring war' and vesting that authority with Congress, the Framers believed that they were granting a monopoly on war initiation to Congress." And: "No one from the founding generation supposed that the President could take the nation to war. Even Alexander Hamilton, an energetic advocate for broad executive power, observed that the President could not take the nation to war."

They cite the Commander-in-Chief clause. But Prakash shows the Commander-in-Chief was never an independent war-maker. Congress held "close to plenary authority over the military and its Commander in Chief" through its power to declare war and regulate the armed forces. The president commands -- after Congress decides.

St. George Tucker, the leading constitutional commentator of the founding era, put it plainly in 1803: "In England the right of making war is in the King. With us the representatives of the people have the right to decide this important question. Happy it is for the people of America that it is so vested." In a monarchy, "the personal claims of the sovereign are confounded with the interests of the nation... who are thus made the victims of a quarrel in which they have no part, until they become principals in it, by their sufferings."

Damon Root at *Reason* reviewed this history: "There are always going to be those cases in which the meaning or applicability of a particular constitutional provision is either unclear or open to more than one interpretation. But this is not one of those cases."

Prakash's verdict: "Modern war-powers practice is at war with the original Constitution."

My opponents say a statute can't override Article II. But Prakash -- a conservative originalist who clerked for Thomas -- says Article II never gave the president war-initiation power to begin with. The resolution doesn't override the Constitution. It restores it.

---

**CONTENTION 2: THE COST**

So what happens when you break that machine? You get wars nobody owns, wars nobody votes for, and wars that never end.

Sarah Burns at RIT documents the pattern. "Congress never authorized the war in Korea; Harry Truman used a U.N. Security Council resolution as legal justification." Congress explicitly voted to oppose the invasion of Cambodia -- "didn't stop Richard Nixon from doing it anyway."

Now my opponents told you the War Powers Resolution proves statutes can't work -- fifty years of non-compliance. But Burns tells you *why* the WPR specifically failed: "It contains many loopholes that presidents have exploited. For example, it allows presidents to engage in military operations without congressional approval for up to 90 days." And they went further: "In 2011, a State Department lawyer argued that airstrikes in Libya could continue beyond the 90-day time limit because there were no ground troops involved. By that logic, any future president could carry out an indefinite bombing campaign with no congressional oversight."

The WPR failed because of loopholes. Our resolution closes them. No 90-day window. No "hostilities" to define down. A clean rule: Congress must vote.

Libya is what the loophole costs. In 2011, President Obama unilaterally committed American forces to a NATO air campaign. The House voted 295 to 123 *against* authorization. The president proceeded anyway. The Atlantic Council analyzed the result: "The Obama administration failed to invest sufficient time or energy into securing authorization for the operation by the US Congress. In doing so, the administration limited its ability to offer critical firepower in the later stages of the conflict." The mission showed "inconsistent and uncertain military and political leadership, in part as a result of legal constraints caused by this lack of Congressional approval."

Now the speed argument. They cite Saigon and the Iran hostage rescue. But genuine emergencies -- repelling an attack, rescuing hostages under fire -- are self-defense, not war initiation. The Framers preserved that power when they changed "make war" to "declare war." Our resolution doesn't touch it. And when the case for war is clear and urgent, Congress moves -- the 2001 AUMF passed in three days. The question isn't speed. It's whether one person decides alone.

Here is the deeper mechanism -- from the American Political Science Review. M. Patrick Hulme at Stanford found an empirical pattern: since Korea, there has "never again been a full-scale war undertaken unilaterally." Presidents are deterred from big wars without Congress -- they'd own the casualties alone. But "smaller uses of force are virtually always undertaken absent Congress's formal blessing."

Why? Because Congress free-rides. Lawmakers want the intervention -- they just don't want their name on it. Hulme: unilateral action "is perhaps often better viewed as a manifestation of congressional power: encouraging the president to intervene, yet refusing to go 'on the record' and hence avoid responsibility if the use of force ends poorly."

That is the forever-war engine. Presidents launch small wars. Congress avoids blame. Wars drift for years. Nobody votes. Nobody is accountable.

The resolution breaks this cycle. It forces the vote the Constitution already requires. The constraint already works for big wars -- no president since Korea has launched one alone. The resolution extends that constraint to the small wars -- the drone strikes, the raids, the Libya campaigns -- that have defined the last seven decades.

They told you a statute can't bind the president. But presidents already avoid big wars without Congress because accountability works. They told you Congress is too slow. Congress passed the AUMF in three days when it mattered -- and true emergencies aren't covered. They told you the WPR failed. We agree -- it failed because of loopholes. Our resolution closes them.

One person should never decide alone to send other people's children to die. The Founders knew it. Libya proved it. This resolution restores it. Vote Pro.
