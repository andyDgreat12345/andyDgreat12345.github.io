*Test topic — not live competitive prep.*

Between rounds, the system rewrote Pro's case: it cut the losing enforcement argument,
found real historical cases where funding statutes constrained presidents, and revived an
argument that had died in Round 1 for lack of examples. Then a fresh pair of teams
debated it. **Pro won 2–1 — a reversal of Round 1.**

An honest caveat the system flags itself: the two rounds used different team
philosophies, so the flipped *result* isn't cleanly caused by the improvements. But the
*reasoning* in each ballot traces straight back to what was added between rounds.

## The ballots

**Lay judge → Pro.** The same failure mode repeated with new teams: Con's case-law depth
read as "dueling court cases that canceled each other out." Pro's evolved crossfire
challenge — "name one case in 235 years where waiting for Congress harmed America" — went
unanswered.

**Flow judge → Pro** *(the flip).* Con's enforcement attack — the one that *won* Round 1 —
failed this time on an internal contradiction: Con's own evidence conceded that formal
authorization does constrain presidents in the biggest cases, which collapsed the "they'll
just ignore it" prong. Pro survived on the enforcement material added after Round 1, and
won the weighing on probability.

**Evidence judge → Con.** And here is the round's real prize. Con executed a **double-turn
on Pro's own flagship card** — a political-science source Pro leaned on — quoting it,
accurately, to argue the resolution was *unnecessary*. Pro's best piece of evidence was
quietly arming the other side.

## Why the landmine matters more than the win

A real opponent at a real tournament might have sprung that double-turn — and Pro would
have had no answer. The system found it in a practice round, for pennies, and wrote a new
rule into the prep: *never use a card in a way its own weakness allows.* That is the whole
point of the loop — the case finds its own holes before anyone else does.
