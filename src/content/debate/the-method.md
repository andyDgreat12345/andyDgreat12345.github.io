Early versions produced text that *looked* like debate and wasn't. The fix was to write
down, explicitly, what separates a real argument from a confident sentence — and to make
the agents follow it. Four standards now govern everything the system writes.

## 1. Arguments, not claims

The single rule everything else serves: **every line has to answer "why?"** A claim is a
statement you assert; an argument is a claim plus the reason it's true plus proof plus why
it matters. Every claim in a case must be able to finish "…because ___." If it can't, it's
an assertion wearing a conclusion, and a good opponent delinks it immediately.

Contentions are built in one fixed shape so nothing is missing:

> **Topic → Uniqueness → Link → Stacked warrants (each carded) → Weighed impacts**

*Uniqueness* — the current state of the world — is not optional. If you can't say why the
harm isn't *already* happening, the argument is dead on its face.

## 2. Evidence you could produce on a challenge

Cards follow the way top competitors actually cut them: a tag that doesn't overclaim, a
full citation *with author qualifications*, and a verbatim body — no paraphrase, no
ellipses in the read portion, no "clipping" (marking as read what you skipped). A quote
pulled from a summary site is never cited as if it were the original. These aren't
niceties; violating them loses rounds and gets reported.

## 3. Speeches that win the back half

Two rules decide most close rounds, and the system enforces both:

- **Collapse.** By the Summary you can't cover everything and win — you go for one path
  completely instead of five weakly.
- **The mirror rule.** Nothing appears in the Final Focus that wasn't in the Summary.
  Judges won't vote on it otherwise, even if the opponent never answered it. It's the most
  common way good teams lose rounds they should win.

And weighing is always explicit and comparative — magnitude, probability, timeframe,
reversibility — because "the team that explains the comparison more clearly takes most
close rounds."

## 4. Reasoning and delivery

Warrants are *stated*, never assumed — the logic chain is spelled out step by step so
there's no unstated leap to attack. After every card the system says "…which means ___,"
so the evidence never argues alone. Speeches are signposted (roadmap, numbered points,
transitions), sentences lead with the claim, filler is cut, and vague intensifiers ("a
lot") are replaced with the number ("hundreds of billions"). Register is matched to the
judge — zero jargon in front of a parent, where "solvency" and "delink" read as evasion.

## Does it actually work?

I tested it the honest way: I had one agent, given *only* these standards and a pile of
evidence, write a fresh case — and a second, adversarial agent grade whether it truly met
each standard. It passed seven of nine criteria, and the two it missed pointed at a real
gap in the standards themselves, which I then closed. The test didn't just check the case;
it improved the rulebook.
