*This is a retrospective on a **test** resolution used to build the system — the April
2026 war-powers topic — not live competitive prep. It's here to show how the learning
loop behaves across rounds.*

The resolution: *the United States should eliminate the President's authority to deploy
military forces abroad without Congressional approval.* I ran two full simulated rounds
and let the system rewrite its own prep in between.

## Round 1 — Con wins, and the pattern appears

Two teams with different philosophies debated it. Con won 2–1, and the three judges split
in a way that turned out to be structural, not random:

- **The lay judge voted Pro** — the "one person shouldn't be able to take the country to
  war alone" story landed.
- **The flow and evidence judges voted Con** — Pro's case had no carded answer to the
  core objection: *a statute won't change what presidents actually do.*

The system's own reflection named the problem: Pro was a lay-panel case losing every
technical panel, because its solvency argument was an assertion with no evidence behind
it. It filed that as the top research priority.

## Between rounds — the loop does its work

The research stage went and found the missing evidence: historical cases where funding
statutes *did* constrain presidents, and — reviving an argument that had died in round 1
for lack of examples — the 1955 Formosa Resolution, where Congress pre-authorized force in
**five days**. These got promoted into the canonical Pro case with a note on exactly why.

## Round 2 — the improved case wins, and finds its own landmine

Round 2 flipped: **Pro won 2–1**, and the flow judge — who had voted Con in round 1 — now
voted Pro, crediting the enforcement evidence the system had added between rounds.

But the most valuable thing wasn't the win. The evidence judge caught Con executing a
**"double-turn" on Pro's own flagship card**: a political-science source Pro relied on
contained a line that, read the other way, argued the resolution was *unnecessary*. Pro's
best piece of evidence was quietly arming the other side.

A real opponent at a real tournament might have sprung that. The system found it in a
practice round, for pennies, and flagged the card as double-edged in the prep with a rule
— "never use a card in a way its own weakness forbids" — so it can't happen again.

## The takeaway

That is the entire point of the loop. A case that simply gets generated is a snapshot. A
case that gets *debated, judged, and rewritten* — round after round — is a case that finds
its own holes before anyone else does. The measure of the system isn't that it wins the
simulated rounds; it's that each round leaves the prep stronger than it found it.
